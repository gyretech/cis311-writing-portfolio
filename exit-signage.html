Modernizing Emergency Exit Signage

A phased investment proposal that connects lifecycle costs, implementation, and evaluation.

Academic proposal. The scope, budget, and savings are planning estimates; this is not an approved agency project.

To: Agency Executive Leadership and Facilities Management

From: Keshwar White

Date: October 4, 2026

Subject: Phased replacement of legacy exit signs with high-efficiency LED units

Purpose and Problem

As a senior IT professional, I evaluate systems by looking beyond purchase price to reliability, maintainability, lifecycle cost, and operational risk. I recommend applying that same approach to emergency exit signs. Facilities that still use older incandescent or fluorescent signs consume electricity continuously and create avoidable maintenance costs. Because exit signs are life-safety equipment, modernization must reduce operating cost without compromising code compliance or emergency reliability.

Kolin (2022) describes a Marine Corps Development and Education Center project in Quantico, Virginia, that replaced conventional exit signs with self-illuminating units. The $97,238 project was expected to save about $37,171 in the first year and achieve a 2.6-year payback. Savings also came from reduced bulb replacement, inventory, transportation, and labor. That lifecycle-cost argument remains relevant even though newer technology gives us additional options.

Proposed Solution

I propose a 100-sign pilot in one or two state office buildings with the highest concentration of legacy exit signs. Facilities staff would first inventory each sign by location, face count, source technology, wattage, age, condition, and backup-power configuration. Eligible fixtures would then be replaced with code-compliant LED exit signs rated at 5 watts per face or less. Federal standards limit covered illuminated exit signs to 5 watts per face, and Department of Energy guidance recommends LED exit signs as a facility energy-efficiency measure (U.S. Department of Energy, 2013). OSHA requires exits to remain clearly visible and properly illuminated, so Facilities should verify each replacement with the authority having jurisdiction before installation (Occupational Safety and Health Administration [OSHA], n.d.).

I recommend LED units instead of tritium-based self-luminous signs. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (2024) states that tritium signs contain radioactive material and cannot be discarded as normal trash; disposal must go through an authorized licensee. LED signs provide low energy use without adding that disposal obligation. This follows the same modernization principle I use in software: simplify the operating model and avoid replacing one maintenance burden with another.

Estimated Savings and Business Value

The pilot should use actual fixture wattage and utility data, but a reasonable planning scenario shows the opportunity. For this academic planning scenario, electricity is assumed to cost $0.2213 per kilowatt-hour. This assumption must be replaced with a verified facility rate before procurement. Replacing 100 continuously operating 40-watt incandescent signs with 5-watt LED signs would reduce annual consumption by approximately 30,660 kilowatt-hours and avoid about $6,785 in electricity costs per year. Savings would be lower where existing fixtures are already fluorescent, which is why the inventory must occur before final procurement.

The business value also includes fewer lamp changes, fewer stocked parts, fewer work orders, and less staff time. Kolin (2022) identified these secondary costs as a meaningful part of the Quantico project's savings. For a public agency, reducing routine maintenance on life-safety equipment also allows staff time to be redirected to higher-priority needs.

Implementation and Evaluation

The pilot can be completed in approximately 8 to 10 weeks. During the first two weeks, Facilities would inventory the existing signs and validate code requirements. Procurement would then obtain competitive pricing for qualified LED units and installation. A licensed contractor would complete the replacement in coordinated sections so exit marking remains continuously available. After installation, Facilities would verify operation and battery backup where applicable, record the installed wattage, and begin a 12-month measurement period.

I recommend a pilot budget ceiling of $20,000, subject to competitive procurement and final scope validation. This is an authorization limit, not an assumed market price. Broader deployment should proceed only if the pilot confirms three outcomes: code compliance, no reduction in emergency reliability, and a projected simple payback of five years or less when verified energy and maintenance savings are combined. If those conditions are met, remaining legacy signs should be prioritized by energy use, age, and maintenance history and converted through normal facility renewal cycles.

Conclusion

Modernizing exit signage is a modest project that can create measurable value across public buildings. The Quantico example demonstrates the lifecycle-cost opportunity, while current federal guidance supports low-power LED technology. A 100-sign pilot lets leadership validate savings with our own data before a broader rollout. I recommend authorizing the inventory and pilot to reduce avoidable energy and maintenance costs while maintaining life-safety reliability.
